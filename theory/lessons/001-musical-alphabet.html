Unit 1 · Notes on the neck

The musical alphabet & the low E string

All of music uses just 12 notes. Today you'll learn what they are, why every fret is one step up the ladder, and how to find any note on your thickest string.

Only 12 notes. That's the whole alphabet.

Music uses the letters A through G. After G it starts over at A. That's seven letter names, called the "natural" notes.

Between most of those letters there's one more note hiding. It's called a sharp (♯, one step up) or a flat (♭, one step down). Same note, two names: A♯ and B♭ are the exact same sound. Which name you use depends on the key you're in, and we'll get to that in Unit 2.

Put it all together and you get 12 notes. Then the pattern repeats, higher or lower.

AA♯B—CC♯DD♯E—FF♯GG♯
The one thing to memorize today There's no sharp between B and C, or between E and F. Those two pairs sit right next to each other. Everywhere else, there's a note in between.

Every fret is one half step

Move up one fret and you've moved up one note in that list. That's called a half step. Two frets is a whole step.

Have a listen. The first pair is a half step (one fret), and the second is a whole step (two frets):

The half step has that tight, tense sound, like the "Jaws" theme. The whole step sounds more open.

Map of the low E string

Your thickest string, played open, is E. Walk up the list one fret at a time and you can name every note on it. Here are the natural notes, the ones you'll use most:

Notice two things:

  • E to F is just one fret, and so is B to C (frets 7 and 8). Those are the two "no sharp" pairs from earlier.
  • The 12th fret is E again, the same note one octave higher. That's why the 12th fret has the double dot. Past fret 12, the whole pattern starts over.
Tap the dots above, or anywhere on the neck. Then play the same thing on your real guitar: open, 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12 on the low E string, saying the note names out loud as you go. E, F, G, A, B, C, D, E.

Why this matters for chords you already play

Most of the open chords you know have their lowest note, the root, on a spot you just learned. The root gives the chord its name:

  • G chord: your ring finger on the 6th string, 3rd fret. That note is G.
  • C chord: ring finger on the 5th string, 3rd fret. That's C. (The 5th string is A, so A, A♯, B, C.)
  • A and E: the open 5th and 6th strings are the roots.

When you start moving chord shapes up the neck later in this course, knowing these notes is what tells you which chord you're playing.

Game: find it fast

Stick to the low E string. The game calls out a note, and you tap where it lives. Aim for 8 out of 10 on the first try.

Today's 5-minute practice

  1. Play up the low E string naturals (0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12), saying each name out loud. Do it 3 times.
  2. Now come back down: 12, 10, 8, 7, 5, 3, 1, 0.
  3. Have someone call out a letter (kids are great at this). Find it on the low E string without counting up from the open string.
