Unit 1 · Notes on the neck

Sharps vs. flats

Last week you learned where the natural notes live on the low E and A strings. Today you'll fill in the gaps: the in-between notes, why each one has two names, and how to find any of them in about two seconds.

One fret, two names

Remember the in-between notes, the ones that sit on the frets between the naturals? Each one can be named from the note below it or the note above it:

  • Sharp (♯) means "one fret higher." F♯ is F, moved up one fret.
  • Flat (♭) means "one fret lower." G♭ is G, moved down one fret.

F♯ and G♭ land on the very same fret, so they're the very same sound. Here are all five in-between notes with both of their names:

C♯ / D♭D♯ / E♭F♯ / G♭G♯ / A♭A♯ / B♭

Same three notes, just walked in opposite directions. The middle note is one sound with two names.

Two-second trick for finding any sharp or flat Find the natural letter you already know, then slide one fret. Sharp? Slide up one (toward your body). Flat? Slide down one (toward the tuning pegs).

Example: where's B♭ on the low E string? B is at fret 7. Flat means down one, so B♭ is at fret 6.

On the low E string they sit at frets 2, 4, 6, 9, 11. On the A string, frets 1, 4, 6, 9, 11. Notice the gaps: there's nothing between 7 and 8 on the low E (B–C), or between 2 and 3 on the A string (B–C again). Those are your old friends, the no-sharp pairs.

Low E string: play fret 2 and say "F sharp, G flat." Then 4 ("G sharp, A flat"), 6, 9 and 11, saying both names each time. Then do the A string: 1, 4, 6, 9, 11. It feels silly saying two names. That's the point: you're teaching your hands that they're the same spot.

So which name do you use?

Both names are right. Which one you see depends on the key the song is in. (A key is the "home base" of a song, the set of notes it's built from. That's all of Unit 2.) For now, here's the plain rule of thumb:

  • Guitar keys use sharps. Songs in G, D, A and E, the keys country guitar lives in, spell their in-between notes as sharps. You'll see F♯m, C♯m and G♯m on those chord sheets.
  • Piano and horn keys use flats. Songs in F, B♭ and E♭ use flats. Church pianists and gospel choirs love these keys, so if you play along with one, expect to see B♭ and E♭.
  • Climbing up, think sharp. Coming down, think flat. Not a law, but it's how most people say it out loud.

One more thing: a key never uses both names for the same note. A song in D says F♯, never G♭. You'll see why in Unit 2.

You already play sharps

Here's a surprise. Three chords you already know each have a sharp tucked inside:

  • D: your middle finger on the high e string, 2nd fret, is F♯. (High e is E, so E, F, F♯.)
  • E: your first finger on the G string, 1st fret, is G♯.
  • A: the note on the B string, 2nd fret, is C♯. (B, C, C♯.)

That sharp is what makes each one a major chord, the bright, happy kind. Lift it down a fret and the chord turns minor. Try D to Dm (xx0231) and hear the F♯ drop to F. We'll dig into that in Unit 4.

Game: sharps and flats

Stay on the low E and A strings. The game mixes both kinds of names. It writes flats as a little b, so "Bb" means B♭. Find the natural, slide one fret, tap.

Today's 5-minute practice

  1. Low E string, frets 2, 4, 6, 9, 11. Say both names for each one. Then the A string: 1, 4, 6, 9, 11. Twice through.
  2. Strum D, then lift your middle finger's note down to make Dm. Back and forth four times. Listen for the F♯ turning into F.
  3. Name game: someone calls out any sharp or flat ("B flat!"). You find the natural letter first, then slide. Kids love catching you when you slide the wrong way.
