Unit 2 · The major scale

Building the G major scale

G is home base for a whole lot of country and gospel. Today you'll build the G major scale yourself with the recipe, see why it needs exactly one sharp, and play it up two different strings.

Build it, one step at a time

Yesterday's recipe: W – W – H – W – W – W – H (whole = 2 frets, half = 1 fret). Start on G and just follow it:

  • G, up a W, is A.
  • A, up a W, is B.
  • B, up an H, is C. (No note between B and C, so the half step lands on a natural.)
  • C, up a W, is D. D, up a W, is E.
  • E, up a W... E to F is only a half step, so we have to go one further: F♯.
  • F♯, up an H, is G. Home.
GABCDEF♯G
The one thing to memorize today G major has one sharp: F♯. Every other note is a plain natural. When a chord sheet says a song is "in G," those are the seven notes the song lives on.

Why F♯ and not G♭?

Back in Lesson 3 you learned F♯ and G♭ are the same sound. So why do we call it F♯ here? There's a simple rule for scales: use each letter once. A through G, one apiece, no skipping, no doubling up.

Our scale already has a G at the start and the end. If we called that 7th note G♭, we'd have two G's and no F at all. Calling it F♯ keeps every letter in line: G, A, B, C, D, E, F. That's why music folks always say "the key of G has an F sharp," never "a G flat."

Play it on the open G string

The easiest place to start: your 3rd string is G when you play it open. Use the open-string fret numbers from yesterday, 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12:

Look for the two dots sitting side by side: frets 4–5 (B to C) and 11–12 (F♯ to G). Those are the two half steps. Every other jump is two frets.

On your guitar, play the G string at 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12, saying the names out loud: "G, A, B, C, D, E, F sharp, G." Then come back down. Go slow; it's a long reach to 11 and 12, so slide your hand up the neck as you go.

Same scale, low E string

Now find the G on your thickest string. You know this one from Lesson 1: 3rd fret. Start there and step it out, 2-2-1-2-2-2-1:

Frets 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15. It's the same pattern of gaps as on the G string, just shifted up three frets because you started three frets higher. Down here it sounds deep and twangy, like a bass line.

Hear the F♯ lean home

Listen to the scale stop one note short, on F♯. It's like a story missing its last line. That 7th note sits a half step under G and practically begs to go up to it. Your ear already knows where home is.

You already play these notes

Here's the payoff. The three chords that run most songs in G, G, C and D, are built only out of G major scale notes:

  • G uses G, B and D.
  • C uses C, E and G.
  • D uses D, F♯ and A. That F♯ is your middle finger on the high e string, 2nd fret. You've been playing the sharp in G major every time you strum a D chord.

That's why G, C and D sound so natural together: they all come from the same seven notes. Next unit, you'll learn how to find those three chords in any key.

Game: G major on the G string

The game calls out a note from the G major scale. Find it on the 3rd string. Open is G; step it out with the recipe if you get stuck.

Today's 5-minute practice

  1. G major on the open G string: 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12, up and back down, saying the names. Three times.
  2. G major on the low E string: 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15. Twice, slow and clean.
  3. Play the scale up but stop on F♯ and hold it. Let the kids (or your own ear) "finish" it by humming the last note. Then play the G and see if they matched.
  4. Strum G – C – D – G and, on the D chord, pick just the high e string by itself. That's the F♯.
