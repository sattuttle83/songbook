Unit 2 · The major scale

Whole steps, half steps & the major scale formula

You've heard "do-re-mi" your whole life. That's the major scale, and it's the backbone of nearly every country and gospel song. Today you'll learn the simple recipe that builds it, and play one up a single string.

Quick refresher: half step, whole step

Back in Lesson 1 you learned that one fret is a half step and two frets is a whole step. Today we put those two to work. We'll write them as H and W.

  • H = half step = move up 1 fret.
  • W = whole step = move up 2 frets.

The recipe: W – W – H – W – W – W – H

A scale is just a set of notes played in order, from a starting note up to the same note an octave higher. The starting note is home base. The major scale is the bright, happy one, the do-re-mi. Every major scale, in every key, follows the same seven steps:

WWHWWWH

On one string, in frets, that's 2 – 2 – 1 – 2 – 2 – 2 – 1. Add those up and you get 12 frets: exactly one octave. You land right back on the note you started with, just higher.

The one thing to memorize today Whole, whole, half. Whole, whole, whole, half. Say it like a little chant: two wholes and a half, then three wholes and a half. That one line builds every major scale there is.

Try the recipe starting on C

Start on C and follow the steps. Watch what happens:

  • C, up a W, is D.
  • D, up a W, is E.
  • E, up an H, is F. (Remember: no note between E and F.)
  • F, up a W, is G. Then a W to A, and a W to B.
  • B, up an H, is C. (No note between B and C either.) Home again.
CDEFGABC

No sharps, no flats. The two half steps in the recipe land right on E–F and B–C, the two pairs you memorized in Lesson 1. That's why C major is the "all naturals" scale, and why those two pairs matter so much.

Play it up one string

Here's C major laid out on the B string (the second-thinnest). Start with your finger on the 1st fret, which is C, and follow the 2-2-1-2-2-2-1 frets up the neck:

Frets 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 13. See the two spots where the dots sit side by side, frets 5–6 and 12–13? Those are the two half steps, E–F and B–C.

On your guitar, play the B string at frets 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 13 with one finger, sliding up. Sing or hum "do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti, do" along with it. If your voice and the guitar match up, you've got it. Then come back down.

Why the recipe matters: it can start anywhere

Here's the beauty of it. Start the same 2-2-1-2-2-2-1 frets on any note and you get that note's major scale. Try it on the open low E string: frets 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12.

Same happy sound, new home base. But tap those dots and look at the names: E major needs four sharps (F♯, G♯, C♯, D♯) to keep the recipe true. The formula decides which notes get a sharp. You don't have to memorize it, you just follow the steps.

What happens if you break the recipe

Now start on G and play only natural notes, no sharps: G A B C D E F G. That's the wrong order of steps near the top: E to F is a half step, F to G is a whole step, backwards from the recipe. Listen to how the end sounds off, like it's not quite home:

Fix it the recipe's way: the 7th note needs to be a whole step above E and a half step below G. That's F♯.

That one F♯ is the whole key of G, the key a big pile of country songs live in. We'll build it properly tomorrow.

Game: step it out on the B string

The game calls out a note from the C major scale. Find it on the B string. Use the recipe if you get stuck: C is fret 1, then count W's and H's up.

Today's 5-minute practice

  1. Say the chant out loud five times: "whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half."
  2. C major on the B string: frets 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 13, up and back down, saying the note names. Three times.
  3. Now start somewhere new: the open A string. Play frets 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12 (that's 2-2-1-2-2-2-1 frets up). Does it sound like do-re-mi? Then the recipe worked. Bonus: have the kids sing along and see who notices if you hit a wrong fret.
