The A string & the octave shortcut

Yesterday you mapped the low E string. Today you'll map the A string right next to it, learn a shortcut that finds the same note higher up the neck, and finish the week by putting it to work in a G-C-D.

Quick look back

Two things from yesterday to keep in your pocket:

  • Every fret is a half step. Two frets is a whole step.
  • B–C and E–F are the only neighbors with no sharp between them. Everywhere else you skip a fret.

That's all you need to map any string. Start on the open note and walk up the alphabet, skipping a fret every time except B→C and E→F.

Map of the A string

The 5th string, played open, is A. Walk it up the same way:

AA♯B—CC♯DD♯E—FF♯GG♯A

So the naturals on the A string sit at open, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12. Look for the two "next-door" pairs again: B to C is frets 2 and 3, and E to F is frets 7 and 8.

A handy link between the two strings The 5th fret of the low E string is A, the same note as the open A string. That's the old way of tuning the A string by ear: fret the low E at 5 and match it.
On your guitar, play the A string naturals: open, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12. Say the names out loud: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A. Then play the 5th fret of the low E and the open A together. They should sound like one note.

Chords that live on the A string

Some of the chords you already play have their root (the note that names the chord) on the A string:

  • C: ring finger on the A string, 3rd fret. That's C.
  • A and Am: the open A string is the root.
  • B7: middle finger on the A string, 2nd fret. That's B.

Notice the × over the low E string on each one. You skip that string because the chord's root is on the A string, and you want the root to be the lowest note.

The octave shortcut

An octave is the same note, just higher (12 frets up, like you saw yesterday). But you don't have to run all the way up to the 12th fret to find one. There's a shape right under your hand:

Two strings up, two frets up From any note on the low E or A string, skip one string toward the floor and move up two frets. That's the same note, one octave higher.
  • G on the low E string, 3rd fret → skip the A string → D string, 5th fret is G again.
  • C on the A string, 3rd fret → skip the D string → G string, 5th fret is C again.

Hear how they blend? Same note, higher up. That's what an octave sounds like.

One catch for later: the shortcut works from the low E and A strings. Once the B string gets involved it's off by a fret. Don't worry about that today.

Put your ring finger on the low E string, 3rd fret (G), and your pinky on the D string, 5th fret. Pick them one after the other, then together. Now move the same shape over a string: ring finger on the A string 3rd fret (C), pinky on the G string 5th fret. Same shape, new note.

Game: the A string

Stay on the A string. Aim for 8 out of 10 on the first try.

Friday: put it to work

Here's a G–C–D–G, the kind of thing a hundred country songs are built on. The root of every chord sits on a spot you know now:

  • G: root on the low E string, 3rd fret.
  • C: root on the A string, 3rd fret.
  • D: root is the open D string (the 4th string).

And to wrap up the week, one more game. This one uses both strings you've learned: low E and A. Any spot that has the note counts.

Today's 5-minute practice

  1. Play up the A string naturals (0, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12), saying each name. Then come back down. Do it twice.
  2. Strum G – C – D – G slowly, four strums each. On the first strum of each chord, pick just the root note first and say its name.
  3. Octave hunt: pick a letter, find it on the low E or A string, then find its octave with the "two strings up, two frets up" shape. Do five letters. (A good one to hand to the kids: they call the letter, you find both.)
